Research

Research Summary

My previous research draws on insurance contexts to understand broader questions in health, behavioral, and public economics. I study multiple forms of insurance, including health insurance, automobile insurance, and public pension. My current research aims to understand the frictions in insurance participation and benefit utilization, as well as to evaluate the impacts of public policy on health outcomes, elderly care, and traffic safety. Methodologically, I am proficient in reduced-form causal identification strategies, and I also complement empirical analyses with economic modeling to situate the findings in a broader theoretical and policy context.

Publication

Migration Incentives and Elderly Health: Evidence from the Hukou Reforms in China

with Wei Huang, Hai Huang, and Wei Zheng

Journal of Development Economics (forthcoming) Paper Link

Abstract: This paper studies the impact of child migration on outcomes of elders who stay behind: consumption, economic participation, health, and mental health. Exploiting variation from the preexisting ties between provinces interacted with the time-varying intensity of regional migration deregulation, we find that increased incentives for internal migration in China resulted in more remittances to rural households, boosting consumption and reducing farm work among the elderly who stay behind. The paper also shows improved healthcare utilization and physical health in these non-migrating elderly populations. However, child migration came with a potential downside: the modest decline in the mental well-being of elderly individuals, possibly due to a lack of informal care and companionship from migrated children. These findings show how internal migration impacts the health and economic wellbeing of elderly individuals in developing countries.

Working Papers

Accident Costs of Traffic Congestion: Evidence from the Driving Restriction Policy in Beijing

with Zining Liu, Fangwen Lu, and Hua Zhou

Abstract: This paper introduces a novel measure of accident-related externalities from urban traffic congestion using two million auto insurance claims from Beijing. We exploit exogenous variation in traffic conditions induced by the license-plate-based driving restriction policy to estimate the impact of traffic congestion on road accidents and quantify the marginal accident cost of congestion. We find negative externalities: following a 10% increase in traffic density, the accident rate becomes 0–12% higher, while accident severity remains largely unchanged. We estimate that a marginal driver generates approximately 11,926 yuan annually in external accident costs during weekday commuting hours. Aggregating across all vehicles within Beijing’s fifth ring road, the total annual external accident cost amounts to 158 million yuan—roughly 2% of the auto insurance premiums. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that accident externalities are disproportionately borne by younger, male owners and vehicles with smaller passenger capacity or higher market values. We highlight the importance of incorporating accident costs into traffic management and congestion pricing policies.

Learning to Use Healthcare: Evidence from a Health Insurance Expansion Program

with Yi Yao, Justin Sydnor, and Wei Zheng

Abstract: This paper examines how learning through a non-trivial healthcare experience spills over to healthcare utilization within a family. Our analysis is based on administrative data from the entire population of disadvantaged households in one county in China, who are automatically eligible for a health insurance expansion program which covers about 95% of their medical expenditure. Exploiting an event-study approach, we show that after a non-trivial health insurance payout for one family member, the medical expenditure of other family members increases promptly and remains higher for over one year. We find evidence consistent with the learning of insurance benefits and healthcare effectiveness behind these spillovers, primarily leading to higher spending on high-cost and life-saving medical treatments that may have been previously underutilized. We suggest positive welfare implications of learning through family experiences, combined with subsidized insurance provision among the disadvantaged population.

Delayed Participation in Social Insurance Expansion: Evidence from China’s New Rural Pension Scheme

with Wei Zheng, and Wei Zhu

SSRN Working Paper 5134771

Abstract: This paper documents the long-term participation in the New Rural Pension Scheme (NRPS) in China, which has been expanded to near-universal coverage. Despite strong monetary incentives, over 30% of rural residents delay their participation. Our theoretical model highlights two distinct dimensions of this delay: purposeful delay caused by risk aversion and procrastination driven by behavioral factors. Exploiting the 60-year-old age discontinuity in benefit structure, we estimate an 18–41% drop in delay time for older residents with a regression discontinuity design. Further heterogeneity analysis reveals that risk aversion drives purposeful delays among residents under the age of 60. In terms of behavioral factors, we find that, despite the minor role of present-bias, procrastinated participation is primarily driven by subjective probability misperception about receiving pension benefits, which is attributed to limited cognition. Additionally, village-level government promotion serves as an effective tool to reduce this misperception, thereby encouraging timely participation.
